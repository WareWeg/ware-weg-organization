Malawi · Wells & boreholes

The year Chisinga got its mornings back

By Tamara Chirwa, Malawi programme officer Published 12 March 8 minute read

Chisinga is a village of 61 households on the edge of the Mzimba plateau in northern Malawi. Until last April its nearest reliable water was the Luwewe river, a 95-minute walk each way down a steep path. In the dry months the river shrinks to pools that cattle share, and families would dig shallow holes in the sand to let water seep in.

When the water committee first met our partner team, they did not ask for a borehole. They asked for a way to stop children missing the first lesson of the day. That question shaped everything that followed.

Choosing the site together

Three sites were surveyed. The one closest to the school had the best yield, but it sat on land owned by one family. The committee spent two meetings agreeing a written arrangement so that the land would stay open to everyone, and the family would get first use of the cattle trough built downstream.

Drilling took four days and reached water at 42 metres. The village provided sand, stone and the labour for the apron and the drainage channel. Before the pump was handed over, a sample went to the district lab and came back clear of bacteria and within limits for fluoride.

“We used to leave before light with the containers. Now the first thing the girls carry in the morning is their school bag.”

Grace Banda, treasurer of the Chisinga water committee

What the numbers show

Our monitoring team visits every water point twice a year and asks a sample of households to keep a simple diary for one week. In Chisinga the time spent collecting water fell from just over three hours a day to around twelve minutes.

The school’s register tells a similar story. Morning attendance for girls in the upper classes rose by 18 percent over the year, and the head teacher has stopped running a second, later register for children who arrived after collecting water.

What didn’t go to plan

In August the pump handle bearing failed. The committee had the spare part money in its cash box, but the area mechanic was at a funeral in another district and the repair took nine days. Since then the committee has trained a second member to do simple repairs, and our partner has added a reserve mechanic to the area roster.

The user fee has also been harder to collect from the poorest households. The committee now accepts payment after harvest, and four households are exempt by agreement.

What comes next

With water close by, three families have started dry-season vegetable plots using wastewater from the drainage channel. The committee wants to add a second tap stand at the school, and has started saving towards its share of the cost.

Our team will be back in March for the next round of tests. You can follow Chisinga’s water point, and every other one we have built, on our open data map.
