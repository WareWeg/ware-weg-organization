Programme 1

Wells & boreholes

Most of the communities we work with already know where water is: a seasonal river, an unprotected hole, a spring that dries in March. We bring hydrogeologists to survey with them, then drill or dig to a source that lasts through the dry season.

  • Hand pumps for villages under 500 people, solar piped schemes with taps for larger ones
  • Every new source tested for bacteria, arsenic and fluoride before handover
  • Rehabilitation of broken pumps built by other organisations, often for a tenth of the cost of a new one

Programme 2

Sanitation

Clean water does little good if the ground around it is contaminated. We train local masons to build durable latrines and sell affordable concrete slabs, so households can build their own and the skills stay in the area.

  • Community-led sanitation sessions that end open defecation village by village
  • Accessible designs with ramps and rails for older and disabled people
  • Pit-emptying services run as small local businesses

Programme 3

Hygiene in schools

Schools are where habits spread fastest. Pupil-run health clubs keep the handwashing stations full, teach younger children and take messages home. Private, lockable toilets with water mean girls no longer miss school during their periods.

  • Handwashing stations at every toilet block and kitchen
  • Menstrual health lessons and washrooms designed with girls and female teachers
  • Health clubs with a small budget the pupils manage themselves

Programme 4

Rainwater harvesting

Where groundwater is too deep, too salty or contaminated with arsenic, the roof is the best source available. We build household jars and larger tanks for schools and clinics, and train families to keep them clean.

  • First-flush diverters that keep roof dirt out of the tank
  • Pond sand filters on Bangladesh’s coast where wells have turned salty

Programme 5

Catchment protection

A water point is only as reliable as the ground that feeds it. With village councils we fence and plant around springs, agree grazing rules and measure flow every season, so sources keep running as rainfall patterns change.

  • Concrete spring boxes with sealed collection chambers
  • Native tree nurseries run by women’s groups
  • Seasonal flow records shared with district water offices

How a project runs

From first meeting to ten-year check

A typical village project takes nine months to build and ten years to finish. These are the stages, in order.

Listen

Our local partner meets the whole village, including women and people with disabilities, to agree what is needed and where.

Months 1–2

Build

Surveying, drilling and construction, with the community providing labour, sand and stone as their share.

Months 3–7

Train

The water committee learns bookkeeping and basic repairs; an area mechanic is trained and stocked with parts.

Months 6–9

Monitor

Twice-yearly visits and water tests for ten years, with results published in our open data map.
